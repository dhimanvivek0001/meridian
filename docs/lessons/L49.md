# Lesson 49: Cold start - can text and photos pick brand-new items?

Script: `scripts/cold_start.py` (about 2 minutes)
Module: M10 NLP / GenAI (lesson 2 of 3)

## The idea in one paragraph

Every recommender so far (L44, L45, L47) needed **sales history**: bestsellers, "bought
together", "you bought it before". A brand-new item has none, so those methods can't see it.
This is the **cold-start problem**. But a new item does have a **description** and a **photo**
from day one. So the question is: if we compare a new item's text and photo with what a customer
already bought, can we guess which new items they will buy? We compare that against simple rules
that don't use embeddings at all.

## The result first

667 new items (first sale ever in the test week, 16-22 Sep 2020). 2,920 customers who bought at
least one of them and have 4 weeks of history. We pick 12 new items for each customer; a "hit"
means they bought at least one of the 12.

| Method | Hit rate | vs random |
|---|---:|---:|
| Random 12 new items | 2.84% | 1.0x |
| Popular product types (same list for everyone) | 2.23% | 0.8x |
| Your own product types | 5.14% | 1.8x |
| Rules: another colour of a product you own, then your types | 6.03% | 2.1x |
| **Text: closest to anything you bought (MiniLM)** | **8.12%** | **2.9x** |
| Photo: closest to anything you bought (CLIP) | 3.42% | 1.2x (photos for only 214 of 667 items, see below) |
| **Text + photo** | **8.49%** | **3.0x** |
| Rules + text | 8.12% | 2.9x (identical to text, see below) |

**Answer: yes.** Comparing descriptions picks new items about **3x better than random** and beats
every rule. The photo adds a little on top.

## Key words

- **Cold start**: recommending something (a new item or a new customer) with no history.
  Collaborative methods ("people who bought X bought Y") need history. **Content-based** methods
  (text, photo, category) don't.
- **Hit rate@12**: the share of customers with at least one purchase among our 12 picks. Same
  metric as M8, but the pool is now only the 667 new items.
- **Max similarity**: a customer's score for a new item is the similarity to the **single most
  similar** item they own. One close match is enough.
- **z-score**: `(x - mean) / std`. It puts two scores (text, photo) on the same scale before we
  average them.

## Honest caveats (read these first)

1. **We knew the launch list.** "New items" = items whose first sale was in the test week. A real
   shop knows which items it launches, so that's fair. But our list only contains new items that
   **sold at least once**; items nobody bought aren't in the pool. That makes random look a little
   better than it would in real life.
2. **Photo subset.** Kaggle rate limits (HTTP 429) stopped the download at **214 of 667** new-item
   photos (32%). The download went in article-ID order, so the 214 are the **lowest IDs**: mostly
   older product lines getting a new colour, and slightly smaller sellers.

   | New items | Count | Units sold in test week | Median units |
   |---|---:|---:|---:|
   | With a photo | 214 | 1,367 | 2 |
   | Without a photo | 453 | 8,197 | 3 |

3. **Owned photos** come from the L46 top-seller subset (1,935 photos), so 86.9% of the customers
   have at least one owned item with a photo.

Because of caveat 2, the photo row in the main table is unfair: the photo method can only pick
among 214 items. Below we add a **like-for-like** test on just those 214.

## Like-for-like: only the 214 new items with a photo

451 customers bought one of the 214 and own at least one photographed item.

| Method | Hit rate |
|---|---:|
| Random 12 | 7.76% |
| Your product types | 13.53% |
| Photo (CLIP) | 21.06% |
| Text (MiniLM) | 23.06% |
| **Text + photo** | **23.73%** |

(All numbers are higher than in the main table because 12 picks out of 214 is easier than 12 out
of 667.) On a level playing field the photo is **almost as good as text** (21.1% vs 23.1%), and
far better than rules. Text still wins, and combining wins slightly.

## A surprise: "rules + text" equals "text"

In L48 we found that **colour variants share the identical description**. So if you own the black
version of a top and the white version is launched, the text similarity is exactly **1.0**, the
maximum possible. Text therefore already ranks "new colour of something you own" first, the same
thing the rule does. Adding the rule changes nothing: 8.12% both times.

Where the purchases come from:

| New-item purchases by these 2,920 customers | Count |
|---|---:|
| New colour of a product one of them owned | 929 |
| Everything else (truly new products) | 3,320 |

So most new-item purchases are **truly new products**, which no rule can match by product code. That's
where the embeddings earn their 8.12% vs the rules' 6.03%.

## Why does "popular product types" lose to random?

Last week's most popular types were things like trousers and T-shirts. But a customer who buys
new items buys **their** kind of product. Showing everyone the same popular types is worse than
random (2.23% vs 2.84%), while "**your** types" nearly doubles random (5.14%). **Personal beats
popular** for new items.

Dictionaries that turn an ID into a row number (customer) or column number (new item), so we can

```python

for c, a in bought_new.join(custs, on="customer_id").iter_rows():
    truth[cidx[c], nidx[a]] = True
```
The answer sheet: `truth[i, j]` is True if customer i bought new item j. On average each row has
**1.46** True values out of 667.

```python
def hit12(score, name, t=None):
    t = truth if t is None else t
    s = score + rng.uniform(0, 1e-6, score.shape)
    top = np.argpartition(-s, 12, axis=1)[:, :12]
    hit = t[np.arange(t.shape[0])[:, None], top].any(axis=1).mean()
```
The scorer used for every method.
- Every method produces a **score matrix** (customer x new item, higher = more likely).
- The tiny random noise (at most 0.000001) breaks ties fairly. Without it, a method that gives
  many items the same score would always pick the lowest column numbers.
- `argpartition(-s, 12)` finds the 12 highest scores per row without fully sorting (faster).
- `t[rows, top]` looks up whether each pick was bought; `.any(axis=1)` = at least one hit;
  `.mean()` = share of customers.
- `t` lets us score a smaller sub-matrix (the like-for-like photo test).

```python
def max_sim(owner_rows, own_emb, new_emb):
    out = np.full((C, N), np.nan, dtype=np.float32)
    sims = own_emb @ new_emb.T
    for i in np.unique(owner_rows):
        out[i] = sims[owner_rows == i].max(axis=0)
    return out
```
- `own_emb`: one embedding per **purchase line** in the history. `owner_rows` says which customer
  each line belongs to.
- `sims = own_emb @ new_emb.T`: similarity of every owned item to every new item (embeddings have
  length 1, so the dot product is the cosine similarity).
- For each customer, take the **column-wise max** over their own items.

A toy example of the max step (made-up numbers): a customer owns 2 items, and there are 3 new items:

| | New A | New B | New C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Owned: black jersey top | 0.91 | 0.40 | 0.35 |
| Owned: denim shorts | 0.30 | 0.38 | 0.82 |
| **Customer score (max)** | **0.91** | 0.40 | **0.82** |

New A and C rank high because each closely matches *one* thing they own. Max rewards "you
bought one of these", whereas an average would blur a mixed wardrobe.

```python
res["random"] = hit12(np.zeros((C, N)), "Random 12 new items")
```
All scores zero, so only the noise decides: a fair random pick. **2.84%**.

```python
types = sorted(set(new["product_type_name"]) | set(hist["product_type_name"]))
tix = {t: k for k, t in enumerate(types)}
last_week = tx.filter(pl.col("sale_date").is_between(pl.date(2020, 9, 9), pl.date(2020, 9, 15))).select("article_id").collect()
type_pop = last_week.join(arts.select(["article_id", "product_type_name"]), on="article_id")["product_type_name"].value_counts()
pop_vec = np.zeros(len(types))
for t, n in type_pop.iter_rows():
    if t in tix:
        pop_vec[tix[t]] = n
item_type = np.array([tix[t] for t in new["product_type_name"]])
res["type_pop"] = hit12(np.tile(pop_vec[item_type], (C, 1)), "Popular product types (same for everyone)")
```
- `pop_vec`: how many units of each product type sold last week (before the cut, no peeking).
- `pop_vec[item_type]`: each new item gets its type's popularity.
- `np.tile(..., (C, 1))` copies that one row for every customer: everyone gets the same list.
  **2.23%**, worse than random.

```python
aff = np.zeros((C, len(types)))
for c, t in hist.select(["customer_id", "product_type_name"]).iter_rows():
    aff[cidx[c], tix[t]] += 1
aff /= aff.sum(1, keepdims=True)
type_score = aff[:, item_type] + 1e-3 * pop_vec[item_type] / pop_vec.max()
```
- `aff` (affinity): for each customer, the share of their purchases in each product type. If 3 of
  your 4 purchases were trousers, trousers get 0.75.
- Each new item gets the customer's share for its type, plus a tiny popularity term as a
  tie-breaker between items of the same type. **5.14%**.

```python
same = np.zeros((C, N))
code_to_items = {}
for j, pc in enumerate(new["product_code"]):
    code_to_items.setdefault(pc, []).append(j)
for c, pc in hist.select(["customer_id", "product_code"]).unique().iter_rows():
    for j in code_to_items.get(pc, []):
        same[cidx[c], j] = 1
rules = same + type_score
```
- `same[i, j] = 1` if customer i owns **another colour of the same product** as new item j.
- 26.8% of the customers own such a product.
- `same + type_score`: same-product items first (score at least 1), then by type. **6.03%**.

```python
mini = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2", device="cpu")
descs = sorted(set(hist["detail_desc"].drop_nulls()) | set(new["detail_desc"].drop_nulls()))
dvec = dict(zip(descs, mini.encode(descs, batch_size=128, normalize_embeddings=True, show_progress_bar=False)))
```
Embed each **distinct** description once (4,846 of them) and keep them in a dictionary. Many
history lines share a description, so this is much faster than embedding every line.

```python
zero = np.zeros(384, dtype=np.float32)
new_txt = np.stack([dvec.get(d, zero) for d in new["detail_desc"].fill_null("")])
h_txt = hist.filter(pl.col("detail_desc").is_not_null())
txt_sim = max_sim(np.array([cidx[c] for c in h_txt["customer_id"]]), np.stack([dvec[d] for d in h_txt["detail_desc"]]), new_txt)
txt_sim = np.nan_to_num(txt_sim, nan=0.0)
```
- New items without a description get an all-zero vector (similarity 0 to everything).
- `max_sim` builds the 2,920 x 667 text score.
- `nan_to_num` turns "no owned description" into 0. **8.12%**, the best single method.

```python
paths = [f"data/images/{a[:3]}/{a}.jpg" for a in new["article_id"]]
has_photo = np.array([Path(p).exists() for p in paths])
model, _, preprocess = open_clip.create_model_and_transforms("ViT-B-32-quickgelu", pretrained="openai")
model.eval()
new_img = np.zeros((N, 512), dtype=np.float32)
with torch.no_grad():
    idx = np.flatnonzero(has_photo)
    for s in range(0, len(idx), 64):
        b = idx[s:s + 64]
        v = model.encode_image(torch.stack([preprocess(Image.open(paths[j]).convert("RGB")) for j in b]))
        new_img[b] = (v / v.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)).numpy()
```
The same CLIP model as L46. Only the 214 new items with a photo on disk are embedded, 64 at a
time. `no_grad()` skips the training bookkeeping (faster, less memory). Each vector is divided by
its length, so dot product = cosine similarity.

```python
old = pl.read_parquet("data/clean/image_embeddings.parquet")
h_img = hist.join(old.with_row_index("r"), on="article_id")
img_sim = max_sim(np.array([cidx[c] for c in h_img["customer_id"]]), old["emb"].to_numpy()[h_img["r"].to_numpy()], new_img)
cover = ~np.isnan(img_sim).all(1)
img_sim = np.nan_to_num(img_sim, nan=0.0)
img_sim[:, ~has_photo] = 0.0
```
- Owned-item photo embeddings come from L46 (`with_row_index` remembers each item's row).
- `cover`: customers with at least one owned photo (86.9%).
- New items without a photo get 0. That's why the photo method scores only **3.42%** over all 667
  items: it is choosing from 214.

```python
z = lambda s: (s - s.mean()) / s.std()
both = np.where(cover[:, None] & has_photo[None, :], (z(txt_sim) + z(img_sim)) / 2, z(txt_sim))
res["rules_text"] = hit12(same * 10 + z(txt_sim), "Rules (same product) + text for the rest")
```
- `z` puts each score on the same scale (mean 0, spread 1), so neither dominates the average.
- `both`: where both a new photo and an owned photo exist, average text and photo; otherwise use
  text only. **8.49%**.
- `same * 10` lifts same-product items above anything text can produce. Identical to text alone,
  because text already gives those items similarity 1.0.

```python
rows_ok = cover & truth[:, has_photo].any(1)
sub = np.ix_(rows_ok, has_photo)
t_sub = truth[sub]
hit12(img_sim[sub], "Photo (CLIP)", t_sub)
```
The like-for-like test. `np.ix_` selects a rectangle of the matrix: the 451 customers who bought a

## What an interviewer might ask

**Q: What is the cold-start problem and how did you handle it?**
A: New items have no interactions, so collaborative signals (popularity, co-purchase, a ranker
trained on sales) can't score them. I used content: the description and photo, compared with
each customer's own purchases. Text max-similarity got 8.12% hit@12 vs 2.84% random, 2.9x.

**Q: Why max similarity rather than average?**
A: Wardrobes are mixed. If you bought jeans and a dress, the average of the two is neither,
and it matches nothing well. Max says "this is very like one thing you bought". Max is the standard
item-to-item approach; a learned customer embedding would be the next step.

**Q: Your photo method scored barely above random. Is CLIP bad?**
A: No, the comparison was unfair: only 214 of 667 new items had a photo (download rate limits),
so it could only pick from a third of the pool. On just those 214 items, photo got 21.1% vs
text 23.1% vs random 7.8%. Always check that methods compete on the same candidates.

**Q: Why didn't adding the "same product, new colour" rule to text help?**
A: Colour variants share identical descriptions, so text similarity is already 1.0 for them.
The rule was redundant. Knowing your data (L48) explains a result that would otherwise look odd.

**Q: What's leaky or optimistic about this evaluation?**
A: The pool only has new items that sold at least once, and we only score customers who bought a new
item. Both make the task easier than in production. The method ranking is still meaningful
because every method faces the same pool. In production I'd use the full launch list and report
hit rate over all active customers.

**Q: How would you put this into the real recommender?**
A: As a candidate source for new items: add each customer's top few new items by text+photo
similarity, then let the ranker (L45) learn how much to trust it, with features like the
similarity and "item age in days". After a week of sales the item stops being cold, and
collaborative features take over.
