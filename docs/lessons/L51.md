# Lesson 51: A simple P&L, and what our earlier lessons are worth in money

Script: `scripts/finance_pnl.py` (about 30 seconds)
Module: M11 Finance + anomaly detection (lesson 1 of 3)

## The idea in one paragraph

A CEO thinks in **profit and loss (P&L)**: money in, money out, what's left. So far each lesson
reported its own kind of number: hit rates, items per day, "full-price units", price-index
units. Here we:

1. build a weekly P&L for Meridian from the transactions;
2. convert the results of four earlier lessons (churn vouchers L17, markdowns L37, ordering L41,
   marketing budget L33) into **one currency and one time frame (per year)**;
3. put them side by side, with every assumption written down.

## The result first

**Annual P&L** (52 full weeks each):

| | Year 1 (from 24 Sep 2018) | Year 2 (from 23 Sep 2019) | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Items sold | 16,724,094 | 14,833,427 | -11.3% |
| Revenue | EUR 271.0m | EUR 246.6m | -9.0% |
| Gross margin (50%) | EUR 135.5m | EUR 123.3m | -9.0% |
| Marketing | EUR 17.5m | EUR 17.5m | 0% |
| **Contribution** | **EUR 118.1m** | **EUR 105.9m** | **-10.3%** |
| Revenue per item | EUR 16.21 | EUR 16.63 | +2.6% |
| Online share of revenue | 74.2% | 77.1% | |

Fewer items sold (-11%), slightly higher price per item (+2.6%), and a shift to online. Marketing is
fixed, so contribution falls faster than revenue (-10.3% vs -9.0%). That's **operating
leverage**: fixed costs magnify a sales drop.

**What our lessons are worth per year, against what the business does today:**

| Lesson / decision | EUR m per year | % of gross margin |
|---|---:|---:|
| L17 churn vouchers, profitable customers only (corrected, see below) | 2.75 | 2.2% |
| L37 per-item clearance markdowns vs no discount | 0.94 | 0.8% |
| L41 newsvendor vs "order the same as last year" | -0.33 | -0.3% (a tie) |
| L33 MMM budget re-split (true effect) | 0.36 | 0.3% |
| **Total** | **3.73** | **3.0%** |

**Context** (not counted in the total):

| | EUR m per year |
|---|---:|
| A flat 50%-off clearance sale instead of no discount | **-9.77** |
| Ordering the "never run out" 90th-percentile case | **-65.4** |
| A perfect forecast (impossible upper bound) | +18.7 |
| What the MMM *promised* for the re-split | +9.40 (truth: 0.36) |

**The big money is in avoiding bad policies, not in clever models.** Our models add about 3% of
gross margin. A blanket sale or a "never run out" ordering policy would destroy several
times that.

## Key words

- **P&L (profit and loss)**: revenue minus costs, top to bottom.
- **Revenue**: what customers paid. Here it's the sum of `price_index` (a disguised price).
- **COGS (cost of goods sold)**: what the clothes cost us to make or buy. We assume 50% of the
  selling price.
- **Gross margin**: revenue - COGS.
- **Contribution**: gross margin - marketing. What's left to pay rent, staff and so on, and then
  profit. We don't model those fixed costs because we have no data for them.
- **Annualise**: convert a result to "per year" (e.g. an 8-week result x 6.5).
- **Counterfactual / baseline**: what would happen otherwise. A gain only means something
  against a stated baseline, ideally what the business does *today*.

## The assumptions (always put these first in a finance table)

| Assumption | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| EUR per price unit | 590 | Prices are disguised. 590 makes the median item (0.0254) about EUR 15. **A guess**, so every result is also printed in price units |
| Gross margin | 50% | Same as L36 and L41 |
| Marketing | EUR 47,962/day | L33's simulated media plan |
| Voucher save rate | 20% | L17 assumption, confirmed 17.7-20.9% by the L24-25 A/B test |
| Voucher cost | 0.02 units (EUR 11.80) | L17 |
| Campaigns / clearances per year | 1 / 2 | Customer value is 12 months of spend; clearances in Jan + Jul |

Is EUR 590 sensible? A check: marketing comes out at **6.4-7.1% of revenue**, a normal level for a
fashion retailer. If the guess were 10x off, marketing would be 0.7% or 70% of revenue, both
implausible.

## A correction to Lesson 17

L17 calculated `expected profit = risk x save rate x SPEND - cost`. But spend is **revenue**.
Keeping a customer only earns us the **margin** on what they buy, not everything they pay.

| | Vouchers sent | Expected gain (units) | EUR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| L17 as written (value = revenue) | 558,456 | 17,173 | 10.1m |
| **Corrected (value = 50% margin)** | **265,103** | **4,664** | **2.75m** |

The gain falls by 73%, and half the vouchers aren't worth sending. This mistake is very common:
**revenue isn't profit**.

All assumptions live in **one dictionary** at the top and are printed first, so anyone reading
the output sees them before any result. `eur()` converts price units to euros. (The script is

```python
tx = pl.scan_parquet("data/clean/transactions.parquet")
assert tx.select(pl.len()).collect().item() == 31_788_324
weekly = (
    tx.with_columns(pl.col("sale_date").dt.truncate("1w").alias("week"))
    .group_by("week").agg(
        pl.col("sale_date").n_unique().alias("days"),
        pl.len().alias("items"),
        pl.col("price_index").sum().alias("revenue"),
        pl.col("price_index").filter(pl.col("sales_channel") == "online").sum().alias("online_rev"),
    )
    .filter(pl.col("days") == 7).sort("week").collect()
)
assert weekly.height == 104
```
- `dt.truncate("1w")` rounds each date down to its Monday, which labels the week.
- Per week: number of distinct days, items (one row = one item), revenue, and online revenue.
- `.filter(days == 7)` drops the partial first and last weeks (the data starts on a Thursday and ends on
  a Tuesday). Comparing a 3-day week with a 7-day week would be a fake drop.
- The assert: exactly 104 full weeks = 2 years.

```python
weekly = weekly.with_columns(
    (pl.col("revenue") * (1 - A["GROSS_MARGIN"])).alias("cogs"),
    (pl.col("revenue") * A["GROSS_MARGIN"]).alias("gross_margin"),
    pl.lit(7 * A["MKT_EUR_DAY"] / A["EUR_PER_UNIT"]).alias("marketing"),
    pl.int_range(pl.len()).truediv(52).floor().cast(pl.Int32).add(1).alias("year"),
```

- Marketing is converted from EUR to price units so all columns share one unit.
- `int_range(...) / 52, floor, + 1` numbers the weeks 0-103 and turns them into Year 1 (weeks 0-51) and

Re-runs L17's rule both ways. For each customer: chance they leave x 20% saved x what they're

Example customer: risk 0.6, 12-month spend 0.50 units.

| Value used | Calculation | Expected profit | Send voucher? |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Revenue | 0.6 x 0.2 x 0.50 - 0.02 | +0.040 | yes |
| Margin | 0.6 x 0.2 x 0.25 - 0.02 | +0.010 | yes, but worth a quarter as much |

A customer with spend 0.30 would get +0.016 on revenue but -0.002 on margin, so **no voucher**.

## What an interviewer might ask

**Q: The prices are disguised. How can you report euros?**
A: I state one conversion assumption (EUR 590 per unit, median item about EUR 15), show every result in
raw units too, and sanity-check it: marketing comes out at 6-7% of revenue, which is plausible for
fashion retail. All the *relative* results (% of margin, which lesson matters most) don't depend
on the guess.

**Q: Why did the L17 voucher value drop by 73%?**
A: L17 valued a saved customer at their revenue; profit is only the margin on it. Using revenue
as value overstates every retention, acquisition or upsell business case. I re-ran the same rule
on margin: 265k vouchers instead of 558k, and EUR 2.75m instead of 10.1m.

**Q: How do you decide what counts in "total impact"?**
A: Against the status quo. Each line compares to what the business does today: no vouchers, no
clearance discount, ordering last year's quantity, the current media split. Losses avoided versus
policies nobody follows ("never run out") are useful context but would inflate the total from
EUR 3.7m to EUR 69m.

**Q: Why does contribution fall faster than revenue?**
A: Operating leverage: marketing is a fixed cost, so a 9% revenue drop becomes a 10.3% contribution
drop. With rent and staff included (also mostly fixed), the effect on operating profit would be much
bigger.

**Q: What's missing from this P&L?**
A: Fixed costs (stores, staff, logistics, IT), returns, VAT, and the real cost price per item
(we assumed 50% everywhere; discounted sales actually have lower margins). It's a contribution
view, enough to compare decisions, not to report earnings.

**Q: Which result would you take to the CEO?**
A: That avoiding bad blanket policies matters more than fine-tuning: a flat 50% sale would cost
EUR 9.8m a year and over-ordering EUR 65m, while all our models together add about EUR 3.7m (3% of
margin). And that the MMM over-promised 26x, so model claims should be tested before money moves.
