# Lesson 52: Anomaly detection on daily sales

Script: `scripts/anomaly_daily.py` (about 1 minute)
Module: M11 Finance + anomaly detection (lesson 2 of 3)

## The idea in one paragraph

A finance or operations team wants an alarm: "something unusual happened to sales today". We split
daily units into **14 series** (store / online x 7 product groups) and try three detectors:

1. a **rolling z-score**, the simple dashboard alert;
2. a **seasonal residual** that knows about weekdays and slow trends;
3. an **Isolation Forest**, a machine-learning outlier detector.

There are no labels saying which days were "really" anomalous, so we check two ways: **plant fake
anomalies** and see who finds them, then **explain the real flagged days by hand** using evidence
(price level, online share, product launches).

## The result first

**Planted test.** 25 fake anomalies (one series doubled or halved for one day). Each method may
flag the same share of the real data:

| Method | Looks at | Flag 1% | Flag 2% | Flag 5% | Flag 10% |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Rolling z (28 days back) | series-day | **3** | **5** | 10 | 14 |
| **Seasonal residual** | series-day | 0 | 3 | **16** | **20** |
| Isolation Forest | whole day | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 |

**Real data, each method's 20 most unusual days:**

| Method | Has an explanation (rule-based) | Agrees with seasonal |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rolling z | 12 / 20 | 10 / 20 |
| Seasonal residual | 16 / 20 (**18 / 20 by hand**) | - |
| Isolation Forest | 17 / 20 | 15 / 20 |

**Most "anomalies" in retail are the calendar:** Christmas, New Year, Easter, Black Friday,
lockdown. The seasonal residual is the best all-rounder. Isolation Forest finds the big
whole-company days but completely misses a single series going wrong.

## Key words

- **Anomaly**: a value far from what we expected. Everything depends on what you *expected*.
- **z-score**: (value - expected) / typical spread. |z| > 3 is the classic alarm.
- **Rolling window**: compute "expected" from nearby days. *Trailing* = past days only (usable
  live). *Centred* = days before and after (only possible looking back, but more accurate).
- **Seasonality**: repeating patterns, here the **weekday** pattern (Saturday differs from
  Tuesday).
- **MAD (median absolute deviation)**: a spread measure that ignores outliers. x 1.4826 makes it
  comparable to a standard deviation.
- **Isolation Forest**: builds random trees that split data at random. Unusual points get
  isolated in very few splits. Score = how quickly a point gets isolated.
- **Alert budget**: how many alarms per period a team can actually look at. Compare detectors at the
  same budget, or one just looks better by shouting more.

holidays and sales are *real* anomalies but **predictable**. A useful production alarm would
include a holiday/promotion calendar, so it only fires on the truly unexpected (like 28 Sep 2019).

**Where is the COVID lockdown?** Store sales fell to zero in March 2020 and stayed there for
weeks. That's not a one-day blip but a **level shift**. The seasonal method's centred window
adjusts to the new level within days, so it mostly flags the transitions (stores reopening on 6 May).
The rolling z keeps firing for weeks after (20 Mar, 2, 4 and 7 May 2020 are among its top 20),
because its "normal" comes from the previous 28 days, which are now a different world. Level
shifts need a different tool: **change-point detection**.

**Isolation Forest's own picks** include 21 Sep 2019 (units 0.56x, online share **39%** vs 75%
usual). Online sales collapsed while stores were normal, which looks like a **website outage**. It
also picks 24 and 31 Dec 2018 (store-heavy shopping days). It's good at "the whole mix of the day
was weird".

## Why the planted test is so hard at 1-2%

A planted anomaly is **one** series out of 14 doubling for one day. A real holiday moves **all 14**
series at once, by a lot (store series on Christmas: z = -43). With only 1-2% of series-days
allowed to alarm (103-205), the real holidays use up the budget. At 5% the seasonal method finds
16/25, and at 10% it finds 20/25.

Why each method behaves as it does:
- **Rolling z** wins at tiny budgets because a sudden jump against the last 28 days is exactly what it
  measures. But it gets confused by weekdays (every Saturday looks a bit odd) and by level shifts.
- **Seasonal residual** removes weekday effects and uses a robust spread, so at 5-10% it's the
  clear winner.
- **Isolation Forest** looks at all 14 numbers of a day together. One series changing by x2 barely
  changes the day's overall shape, so it finds 1/25. Use it for "is this day unusual overall?", not
  "which series broke?".

(Illustrative units; the point is that doublings look the same.)

```python
def roll(x, w, fn, center):
    out = np.full_like(x, np.nan)
    for i in range(len(x)):
        lo, hi = (i - w // 2, i + w // 2 + 1) if center else (i - w, i)
        if lo >= 0 and hi <= len(x):
            out[i] = fn(x[lo:hi], axis=0)
    return out
```
A small helper: for each day, apply `fn` (mean, std or median) to a window of rows. A **trailing** window
is days i-w to i-1 (past only). A **centred** window is w//2 days on each side. Where the window
doesn't fit (the edges), the result stays NaN.

```python
def z_rolling(L):
    m, s = roll(L, 28, np.mean, False), roll(L, 28, np.std, False)
    return (L - m) / np.maximum(s, 0.05)
```
**Method A.** Today vs the mean of the previous 28 days, divided by their standard deviation.
`np.maximum(s, 0.05)` stops division by a near-zero spread (e.g. a store series stuck at 0 during
lockdown).

```python
def z_seasonal(L):
    base = roll(L, 29, np.median, True)
    r = L - base
    for w in range(7):
        r[wday == w] -= np.nanmedian(r[wday == w], axis=0)
    mad = 1.4826 * np.nanmedian(np.abs(r - np.nanmedian(r, axis=0)), axis=0)
    return r / np.maximum(mad, 0.05)
```
**Method B**, in three steps:
1. `base`: the centred 29-day **median**, a smooth level that a single spike can't drag up (a mean
   would be dragged).
2. Remove each series' typical **weekday** effect. If online Upper body is always 10% higher on
   Sundays, subtract that.
3. Divide by the **MAD** (robust spread), so the many big holidays don't inflate "normal noise" the
   way a standard deviation would.

Example, Black Friday 2019, online Underwear: the residual is so large that z = **+15.0**.

```python
def iforest(L):
    z = np.nan_to_num(z_seasonal(L))
    f = IsolationForest(n_estimators=300, random_state=SEED).fit(z)
    return -f.score_samples(z)
```
**Method C.** Each day is one row of 14 seasonal residuals. The forest (300 random trees,
fixed seed) learns what typical days look like. `score_samples` is higher for normal points, so we
negate it to make higher = more unusual.

```python
def day_scores(L):
    za, zb = z_rolling(L), z_seasonal(L)
    ok = ~np.isnan(zb).any(1) & ~np.isnan(za).any(1)
    sc = {"Rolling z (28 days back)": np.abs(za).max(1), "Seasonal residual": np.abs(zb).max(1), "Isolation Forest": iforest(L)}
    return {k: np.where(ok, v, -np.inf) for k, v in sc.items()}, za, zb
```
Turns the series scores into **one score per day**: the biggest |z| among the 14 series. Edge days
without a full window get -infinity, so they're never picked.

## What an interviewer might ask

**Q: How do you evaluate an anomaly detector without labels?**
A: Two ways. Plant synthetic anomalies with a known location and size, and measure recall at a
fixed alert budget. Then hand-review a sample of real flags for precision: here 18/20 of the
seasonal method's flags had a clear cause.

**Q: Why compare at a fixed alert budget?**
A: Any detector finds more by alarming more. What's limited is the team's attention: say 2-5% of
series-days. At equal volume, seasonal residual found 16/25 planted anomalies at 5% vs 10/25 for
rolling z.

**Q: Why did Isolation Forest do so badly on the planted test?**
A: It scores the whole day as a 14-number vector. One series doubling barely changes the day's
overall shape, so it's diluted. It's good for "whole day weird" (e.g. a website outage on 21 Sep 2019,
when online share fell to 39%), not for finding which series broke. Pick the model to match the
question.

**Q: Your top anomalies are Christmas and Black Friday. Is that useful?**
A: They're correct but predictable. In production I'd add a holiday and promotion calendar as
features (or exclude known event days), so alerts surface the truly unexpected: 28 Sep 2019, a 5.4x
online surge at normal prices that nobody can explain from the data alone.

**Q: How do you handle COVID lockdown in this kind of system?**
A: It's a level shift, not a spike. A trailing-window alarm keeps firing for weeks; a centred
or seasonal model adapts. Better: change-point detection to declare "new regime" once, then
reset the baseline.

**Q: Why log units and a median/MAD instead of mean/std?**
A: The log makes a doubling the same size for big and small series. The median and MAD are robust:
the 2-3% of extreme days don't inflate "normal", so they stay visible as outliers.
