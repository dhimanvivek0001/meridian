## The idea in one paragraph

L52 looked for unusual **days**. Here we look for unusual **individual purchases and customers**,
the kind of thing a loss-prevention or fraud team cares about:

- prices far from what everyone else paid;
- one person buying many identical items;
- enormous baskets.

We use simple, explainable **rules** and compare them with a machine-learning **Isolation Forest**
over customers. There are no fraud labels, so we **check precision by hand**: draw 12 random flags
from each detector, read the evidence, and decide whether each is worth a reviewer's time.

## The result first

| Detector | Flagged | Hand check: worth review |
|---|---:|---:|
| Price **far below** what others paid (< 0.3x) | 14,911 lines | **7 / 9** |
| Price **far above** what others paid (> 3x) | 4,552 lines | **0 / 3** |
| Bulk: 10+ of one article, one customer, one day | 4,430 lines (3,231 customers) | 5 / 12 |
| Huge basket: 60+ items in a day | 593 baskets | 7 / 12 |
| Isolation Forest, top 500 customers | 500 customers | 8 / 12 |
| **All 48 samples** | | **27 / 48 = 56%** |

The most interesting single finding: **5,406 lines at exactly 80% off**, spread over **4,888
different customers** (all the 80%-off cases in our sample were store purchases where at most one other customer paid that price that day).
That's not a few fraudsters; it looks like a **till-level discount scheme** (staff discount? damaged
goods? price override?). It's the first thing to ask loss prevention about.

## Key words

- **Reference price**: what other people paid for the same thing at the same time. A price is only
  "odd" relative to a reference.
- **Ratio**: price / reference. 0.20 = paid 20% of what others paid (80% off).
- **p10 / p90**: the 10th and 90th percentile of an article's prices over 2 years, its usual
  low and high price.
- **Precision**: of the things we flagged, the share that are really worth flagging. Recall (did we
  catch all of them?) needs labels we don't have.
- **Rubric**: the rules for the hand check, written down **before** looking at the samples, so we can't
  bend the judgement afterwards.
- **Isolation Forest**: an outlier detector (L52) that isolates unusual rows with few random splits.
  Here each row is one customer.

## The hand-check rubric (fixed before reading the samples)

| Flag | "Worth review" if... | "Benign" if... |
|---|---|---|
| Price | below half the article's own usual low price (< 0.5 x p10), with nobody else paying it | the price is inside the article's normal range (e.g. full price in a markdown week) |
| Bulk | 10+ identical **fashion** items (sweater, trousers, skirt), a one-off account, or repeat bulk days | cheap **basics** (tees, tank tops, scrunchies): stocking up, team kits |
| Basket | under a third of lines are distinct articles, or an account with 3 or fewer shopping days | many different articles: a big haul |
| Customer (IF) | one of the patterns above | just a heavy, loyal shopper |

**B. Bulk** (10+ units of one article): 5/12 worth review. Sweater x10, shorts x12 by a one-day
account, work shirts x14 by a one-day account (business buyer?), wide trousers x16, and skirt x27 by
a customer with bulk buys on **5 different days** (reseller-like). The other 7 were cheap basics
(tank tops at 0.0027, T-shirts, a scrunchie): people stocking up.

**C. Huge baskets** (60+ items): 7/12. For example 143 items but only **10 different articles**, or
114 items from an account with 3 shopping days. The 5 benign ones had 22-62 different articles
(at least a third of their lines distinct) from regular customers: a big but normal haul (one on Black Friday 2018, in a store).

**D. Isolation Forest customers**: 8/12. It finds customers like "63 items over 2 days, 52 of the
same article" or "4 items in a store at 37% of the usual price". The 4 false alarms: a **very loyal
heavy shopper** (282 items over 65 days), two small store customers with some deep discounts, and a
one-off online order of 8 identical items (below our bulk threshold; could be sizes or gifts).
That's typical: an outlier detector flags **unusual**, not **bad**; very loyal customers are unusual too.

## Rules vs model

| | Rules | Isolation Forest |
|---|---|---|
| Explainable? | Yes ("10+ of the same item") | Needs the feature table to explain |
| Found | Clear single patterns | Combinations (1 day + many identical items + all online) |
| Overlap | | 260 of its 500 customers also hit a bulk/basket rule; 66 the price rule |
| False alarms | Basics; full-price-in-markdown-week | Heavy loyal shoppers |

In practice you'd use both: rules for known patterns (easy to explain to an auditor) and the model
to suggest new patterns, which you then turn into rules.

```python
SEED = 53

LOW, HIGH = 0.3, 3.0
```

All thresholds are named at the top. `MIN_REF = 10`: we only judge a price if at least 10 sales that
week give a reliable "what others paid". `BASKET = 60` is about the top 0.01% of shopping days (the
99.99th percentile is 53).

```python
ref = tx.group_by(["article_id", "week", "sales_channel"]).agg(pl.col("price_index").median().alias("ref"), pl.len().alias("n_ref"))
priced = tx.join(ref, on=["article_id", "week", "sales_channel"]).with_columns((pl.col("price_index") / pl.col("ref")).alias("ratio"))
pf = priced.filter((pl.col("n_ref") >= MIN_REF) & ((pl.col("ratio") < LOW) | (pl.col("ratio") > HIGH))).collect()
```
- `ref`: the **median** price for each article x week x channel (the median ignores a few odd prices).
- `ratio`: each line's price / that reference.
- Keep the lines below 0.3x or above 3x.

**Why not compare with the article's all-time median?** We tried: 411,170 lines came out "> 2x", because
markdowns drag an article's long-run median down. Comparing with the **same week** removes that.

| | Lines flagged |
|---|---:|
| vs all-time article median (< 0.3x or > 2x) | 215,224 + 411,170 |
| **vs same article, same week, same channel (< 0.3x or > 3x)** | **19,463** |


many did, it's a systematic price (e.g. a markdown starting mid-week). Only **7.0%** of flags have 3+

## What an interviewer might ask

**Q: How do you measure an anomaly detector without fraud labels?**
A: Precision by hand review: sample flags at random, judge them with a rubric written before
looking, and report the share worth reviewing. Here 27/48 = 56% overall, but 7/9 for low prices and 0/3
for high prices, which told me to drop one rule. Recall needs labels (confirmed fraud cases) or
planted anomalies (L52).

**Q: Why compare a price with the same week, not the article's normal price?**
A: Prices change over an item's life (markdowns). Against the all-time median, 626k lines looked
odd; against the same article-week-channel, 19k. Even then, "high" flags were customers paying the
full price in a markdown week. The right reference is "what others paid at the same time".

**Q: You found 5,406 sales at exactly 80% off. Fraud?**
A: Probably not individual fraud: 4,888 different customers, the top 10 have 1% of them, and nobody
else paid that price that day. A consistent, widespread pattern suggests a process: a staff
discount, damaged-goods discount, or manual price override at the till. I'd take it to loss
prevention as a question with examples, not as an accusation.

**Q: Rules or Isolation Forest?**
A: Both. Rules are explainable and auditable; the forest finds combinations nobody wrote a rule for
(one-day online accounts buying many copies). Half the forest's top 500 overlapped with the rules.
Its false alarms are very loyal heavy shoppers: unusual isn't the same as bad.

**Q: What data would make this much better?**
A: Sizes and returns. Ten units of the same article might be five sizes ordered to try on and
returned (bracketing), which is costly but not fraud. Also store IDs (a till-override pattern is
usually one store or one employee) and payment data (many accounts with one card = reseller ring).

**Q: The hand check is your own judgement. Isn't that biased?**
A: That's why the rubric was fixed first and every case is shown with its evidence, so someone else
can re-label it. In a real team two people would label independently and we'd report their agreement.
